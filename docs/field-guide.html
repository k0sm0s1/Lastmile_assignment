1What we were given

A point cloud, and two traps hidden inside it.

The file map_ros_cloud.ply is a point cloud: 2,338,923 separate 3D dots, each with a position (x, y, z), a colour (r, g, b) and a surface normal (which way the surface faces). It came out of a SLAM system (the header says "PCL generated"), meaning someone walked a scanner through a building and the software stitched the readings together.

The first thing to check with any unfamiliar data is what it actually is, not what the filename suggests. The header says element face 0: there are zero triangles. A mesh would have faces. So this is not something you can "import" into Gazebo. It has to be reconstructed into a surface first. That realisation drives the whole of Task 1.

Trap 1: the floor isn't one flat plane

SLAM drifts slowly as it builds a map, so the "floor" in the final cloud bends by roughly ±10 cm across the building. Any method that says "height above z = 0" will call some real floor an obstacle. Our fix was to measure height above the local floor (section 6).

Trap 2: ghost points at the start of the scan

The origin (0, 0) is where the scanner started, and it lingered there. People walking past and sensor noise left a sparse haze of points at every height. A naive rule like "any point between 8 cm and 1.8 m high = wall" turns that haze into a solid black block. Together, traps 1 and 2 made the first version of the map claim that (0, 0) was inside a wall, which was false and caused a lot of the early trouble.

2Cleaning the cloud · preprocess_cloud.py

Make it smaller, remove stray points, and put the floor at exactly z = 0.

  1. Voxel downsample at 3 cm. Imagine filling space with 3 cm cubes and replacing all points in each cube with their average. 2.3 M points become roughly 10× fewer, with no visible loss of wall or furniture detail. Everything after this runs faster.
  2. Statistical outlier removal (20 neighbours, 2.0 standard deviations). For each point, look at the average distance to its 20 nearest neighbours. Points much further from their neighbours than typical are isolated specks, so they're dropped.
  3. RANSAC floor fit. RANSAC (Random Sample Consensus) picks 3 random points, fits a plane through them, and counts how many other points lie within 2 cm of it. It repeats this 2,000 times and keeps the plane with the most supporters. Outliers can't fool it, because a bad plane has few supporters.
  4. Rotate so the floor normal points straight up, then shift so the floor is at z = 0. The rotation uses Rodrigues' formula: R = I + [v]× + [v]×² · (1 − c)/s², where v is the cross product of the floor normal with the z-axis, s its length and c the dot product. The floor height is found as the most common z value (histogram peak) among the lowest 40% of points.
  5. Save the transform to data/floor_transform.json, so every later script puts points in exactly the same frame.
Why only the bottom 40% for RANSAC?In a corridor, walls have far more surface area than the floor. Run RANSAC on everything and it may "find" a wall as the dominant plane. Restricting candidates to the lowest 40% of heights guarantees it's looking at the floor.
Smart detailSaving the transform instead of just the cleaned cloud means the map script can read the raw PLY and apply the same rotation, so the map and the Gazebo mesh line up perfectly without needing open3d or the intermediate file.

Result: the floor normal was only 1.3° off vertical and the offset was about 1.9 cm. The scan was already close to level, but now it's exact.

3From points to a surface · cloud_to_mesh.py

Gazebo needs triangles. We have dots. This is the reconstruction step.

  1. Re-estimate normals consistently. Each point's normal says which side of the surface is "outside". The shipped normals weren't guaranteed consistent (some could point inward), and Poisson needs them consistent, so we recompute them from the 30 nearest neighbours and orient them to agree with each other.
  2. Poisson surface reconstruction, depth 9. Picture the points as samples of an "inside vs outside" boundary. Poisson solves for a smooth 3D function whose boundary best matches all the points and normals, then extracts that boundary as triangles. Depth 9 means a 2⁹ = 512-cell grid per axis, a good balance of detail and time.
  3. Trim low-density vertices (bottom 3%). Poisson always produces a closed, watertight surface, even across holes where nothing was scanned (open doors, gaps). It reports a "density" for each vertex, meaning how many real points supported it. Low density = invented surface. We cut those out.
  4. Clean up degenerate, duplicated and non-manifold triangles, which crash or slow physics engines.
  5. Decimate to 150,000 triangles with quadric error decimation, which merges triangles where it changes the shape least. Physics runs in real time; millions of triangles would not.
Why Poisson and not a simpler method?Simpler methods (ball-pivoting, greedy triangulation) connect nearby points directly and leave a surface full of holes from a walked-path scan. Poisson gives a smooth, closed surface, and its density output tells us exactly where it guessed, so we can remove the guesses. We get Poisson's smoothness without its main weakness.
ScaleNo scaling step is needed anywhere. The cloud is already in metres, and the SDF uses <scale>1 1 1</scale>, which satisfies "1 unit = 1 metre" directly.

4What you see vs what you bump into · make_collision_mesh.py

The fix for a robot that fell through the floor, then couldn't move.

Every Gazebo object has two geometries. The visual is what the camera draws. The collision is what physics uses for contacts, and what the lidar's rays hit. They don't have to be the same, and here they aren't.

The script removes two kinds of triangles from the collision copy:

  • Near-horizontal triangles below 0.25 m (the normal's z component is above 0.7, so the triangle is facing up or down). That is the floor.
  • Anything entirely below 0.12 m: floor clutter and the ragged bottom edge of walls.

It kept 127,078 of 149,999 triangles. The model then gets a flat analytic ground plane at z = 0 as a second collision. Walls still collide with the robot and still reflect the lidar (mounted at about 0.27 m), so nothing about sensing changes.

Smart detailBoth mesh scripts regenerate their output byte-for-byte from the inputs. Anyone can rerun the pipeline and diff the result against what's committed.

6Turning 3D into a 2D map · cloud_to_occupancy_grid.py

The single most important file for navigation quality.

Nav2 plans on a flat grid. Each cell is free (white, 254), occupied (black, 0) or unknown (grey, 205). The grid is stored as a .pgm image plus a .yaml saying how big a pixel is (0.05 m) and where pixel (0, 0) sits in the world (origin: [-5.1899, -6.2513]). The assignment allowed either a live 3D-to-2D projection in the costmap or a pre-generated map. We pre-generate it, because the world never changes, and a one-time projection is simpler, faster and easy to inspect.

The v2 algorithm, step by step

  1. Load the raw PLY with numpy (a hand-written parser that reads the header and maps the binary data into arrays) and apply floor_transform.json. No open3d is needed.
  2. Estimate the local floor. Split the site into 0.25 m tiles. In each tile take the 10th-percentile height of the low points (robust to a few stray points below the floor). Fill empty tiles from their nearest neighbour, then median-smooth. Now every point gets a height above its local floor.
  3. Require real vertical structure for an obstacle. In each 5 cm cell, count points between 0.12 m and 1.60 m above the local floor, and count how many distinct 5 cm height bands they occupy. Occupied only if there are at least 40 points spread over at least 8 bands (a 40 cm-tall structure), or at least 150 points. A wall has hundreds of points stacked floor to ceiling. Ghost haze has a few scattered points, so it fails the test.
  4. Clean the mask with morphological opening (removes lone pixels) and closing (seals hairline gaps in walls).
  5. Free = floor we actually saw. Cells with points within ±6 cm of the local floor are "observed floor". Fill small holes, subtract obstacles, and keep only the region connected to (0, 0). Everything else stays unknown.
  6. Write the pgm + yaml in the same grid frame as v1, so nothing else had to change.
Why "observed floor = free" mattersThe scan has open ends with no outer walls. v1's flood fill ran through those gaps out to the edge of the image, so the planner could route through space nobody scanned. v2 only calls a cell free if floor was actually seen there, and the planner runs with allow_unknown: false.

Final map: 529 × 367 cells at 5 cm; 32,639 free, 4,872 occupied, the rest unknown. The script regenerates the committed map byte-for-byte.

The biggest correction in the projectThe earlier version concluded "(0, 0, 0) is inside a wall" and moved the spawn to (−0.24, 1.10). That spawn was actually against a real wall, 7 cm from it. After v2, and a ray-cast of the Gazebo mesh from the origin that found nothing within 0.6 m, we moved the robot back to (0, 0, 0), exactly as the assignment asks. Check assumptions against a second, independent source.

7Frames & localization: how the robot knows where it is

The TF tree, AMCL, and why each link has a different owner.

ROS describes positions with frames (coordinate systems) joined by transforms. The assignment specifically asks for map → odom → base_link to be alive and stable. Here's the full chain and who publishes each link:

How AMCL works, briefly

AMCL (Adaptive Monte Carlo Localization) keeps 500 to 2,000 guesses ("particles") of where the robot might be. On every laser scan:

  1. Move every particle by the odometry motion, plus some noise.
  2. Score each particle: if the robot were there, would the laser hits land on walls in the map? We use the likelihood field model, which pre-computes each cell's distance to the nearest wall, so scoring is fast.
  3. Resample: keep more copies of high-scoring particles and drop low ones. The cloud of guesses collapses onto the true pose.
  4. Publish the best estimate as map → odom, i.e. "how wrong is odometry right now".

AMCL is seeded at (0, 0, 0) via set_initial_pose: true, so it starts confident. It republishes map → odom at about 5 Hz even when the robot stands still, stamped 1 s in the future (transform_tolerance) so other nodes never find it slightly stale.

The lidar sees the robot itselfThe scan had returns at 0.12–0.16 m in several directions. A ray-cast of the Gazebo mesh from the lidar position found nothing that close, so these were hits on the Waffle's own chassis and camera mount. We set laser_min_range: 0.3 for AMCL (and obstacle_min_range: 0.3 while the costmaps used the laser), so the robot doesn't treat itself as an obstacle.
Measured accuracyCompared with Gazebo's ground-truth pose at each goal arrival, AMCL was off by 5–16 cm. For a robot 44 cm wide in a 2 m corridor, that's comfortably good.

8How Nav2 actually moves the robot

From "go to (8, 0)" to wheel speeds, 10 times per second.

Nav2 is a set of cooperating servers, each switched on in order by a lifecycle manager. That's why the dashboard shows each server's state ("active").

Costmaps: where the robot thinks it may drive

A costmap is a grid of "how bad is it to be here" (0 to 254). There are two: the global one covers the whole map for planning; the local one is a 3 × 3 m window that moves with the robot, for the controller. Both use:

  • static_layer: walls from our map.
  • inflation_layer: cost fades out over 0.55 m around walls, so paths keep a safety margin from walls instead of scraping them. The robot's radius is 0.22 m.
Why no live laser obstacle layerNormally the costmaps also add laser hits in real time. In this setup, that obstacle layer froze its costmap at start-up: its TF message filter locked up, so the costmap's copy of the transforms stopped updating (we saw the footprint stuck at t = 37.8 s while the clock read 156 s). With stale transforms, every goal failed to convert into the robot's frame, and Humble's controller then compared against an all-zero pose and announced "Reached the goal!" without moving. It happened reliably on cold starts in 6 of 6 tries. Using static maps only fixed it: 0 failures in the retests. The trade-off is that the robot won't dodge objects that aren't in the scan. For a fixed, scanned world, that's acceptable, and it's documented.
False "recoveries" from a 20 ms timeoutbt_navigator waits only 20 ms by default for the planner or controller to acknowledge a request. On a loaded laptop that's often too short, so the tree counted it as a failure and ran a recovery, giving stop-and-go near x ≈ 4. Raising it to 500 ms (and dropping the controller from 20 Hz, which it kept missing, to 10 Hz) took that corridor leg from 5 recoveries to 0.

9The custom node · robot_state_manager

Task 3. Python (rclpy). File: src/robot_state_manager/robot_state_manager/robot_state_manager_node.py.

One node with four jobs from the assignment, plus two extras for the dashboard.

1 · Position publisher

Every 0.1 s it looks up the transform map → base_footprint, which is AMCL's correction composed with odometry: the robot's best-known position in the building. It publishes [x, y, z] as std_msgs/Float32MultiArray. If TF isn't available yet it falls back to /odom, and until either exists it publishes nothing rather than a fake (0, 0, 0).

Why a wall-clock timer?The simulation runs at about 0.8× real time. A timer on simulated time ticks 10 times per simulated second, which measured only 7.6 Hz of real time. The assignment says 10 Hz, and anyone subscribing expects real seconds, so the timer uses the steady (wall) clock. Measured: 10.0–10.1 Hz.
Why an array and not a custom message?The assignment explicitly allows "a custom message or a simple array". A custom message needs a separate interface package with its own build type, which is a lot of ceremony for three numbers.

2 & 3 · Goal receiver + Nav2 action client

It listens on /robot/next_waypoint for a geometry_msgs/PoseStamped, the exact type the Nav2 goal wants, so nothing gets lost converting between formats. A blank frame becomes "map", an all-zero quaternion becomes "facing +x", and the timestamp is set to 0 ("use the latest transform"). Then it sends a NavigateToPose goal through an rclpy ActionClient.

"MapsToPose"The assignment text says MapsToPose. There is no such action in Nav2. The action that drives to a pose is nav2_msgs/action/NavigateToPose, so we treated it as a typo and say so in the README.
stateDiagram-v2
  [*] --> idle
  idle --> sending: waypoint received
  sending --> server_unavailable: Nav2 not up (2 s)
  sending --> rejected: goal refused
  sending --> navigating: accepted
  navigating --> navigating: feedback (distance, recoveries)
  navigating --> sending: new waypoint (preempts)
  navigating --> succeeded: result + verified within 0.5 m
  navigating --> aborted: Nav2 gave up
  navigating --> canceled: /robot/cancel
  succeeded --> sending: next waypoint
  aborted --> sending: next waypoint

Each goal gets an increasing id. Results from an older, preempted goal are ignored, so a late "aborted" from goal 3 can't overwrite goal 4's state.

Don't trust "succeeded" blindlyWhen Nav2 reports success, the node measures its own localized distance to the goal. More than 0.5 m away means it logs an ERROR and marks the result unverified. This is exactly what caught the frozen-costmap bug: "succeeded" while 15.67 m away.

4 · Command monitor

It subscribes to /cmd_vel, the velocity Nav2's controller sends to the wheels. Once a second it echoes the command, and it logs a WARN whenever |v| > linear_velocity_threshold or |ω| > angular_velocity_threshold. The warnings are throttled to 2 per second so the log stays readable. Defaults are 0.20 m/s and 0.80 rad/s, set just under the robot's limits (0.22, 1.0) so the monitor visibly fires in the demo. Change them at launch:

ros2 launch robot_state_manager robot_state_manager.launch.py \
    linear_velocity_threshold:=0.15 angular_velocity_threshold:=0.5

Sample log lines from a real run:

[WARN] [robot_state_manager]: [cmd_vel] linear speed 0.220 m/s exceeds threshold 0.20 m/s
[WARN] [robot_state_manager]: [cmd_vel] angular speed 1.000 rad/s exceeds threshold 0.80 rad/s (turning fast)

Extras

  • /robot/nav_status (JSON string, 5 Hz and on every change): state, goal, distance remaining, recoveries, nav time, final error, verified flag, latest cmd_vel with threshold flags. The dashboard reads this.
  • /robot/cancel (std_msgs/Empty) cancels the active goal.

10The dashboard · lastmile_dashboard

A live web page for the robot, with nothing extra to install.

Design choices

  • No rosbridge, no pip installs. The usual route (rosbridge + roslibjs) needs extra packages. Python's built-in http.server plus SSE did the job, and a ~15-line PNG encoder (zlib + struct) turns /map into an image the browser can show.
  • Lidar points in map coordinates. Each scan is converted to map x/y through TF, then drawn on the map, so you can see the laser lining up with the walls. That's a visual check that localization is right.
  • Health from the source. "AMCL localized" means the map → odom transform exists and is fresh. "Nav2 active" comes from asking each server its lifecycle state every 2 s.
  • History from the node, not the page. Every goal id the Task 3 node handles appears, whether it came from the dashboard or the command line.

Using it: click the map to send a goal (drag to set the arrival heading), scroll to zoom, right-drag to pan, double-click to reset. Preset buttons send common goals. "Set pose" mode re-seeds AMCL by clicking where the robot really is.

11The demo recorder

Proof on video, recorded without any screen capture.

WSL had no ffmpeg and no pip, but OpenCV was available. So instead of recording the screen, record_demo.py uses a camera inside Gazebo:

  1. A gravity-free camera model (demo_camera) is spawned in the world.
  2. 20 times a second the recorder reads the robot's odometry and moves the camera 1.6 m behind and 1.25 m above it through Gazebo's /gazebo/set_entity_state service. The camera's heading is smoothed so spins don't whip the view.
  3. The camera's images arrive on /demo_cam/chase/image_raw. The recorder draws the overlay with OpenCV and writes an MP4 at 10 fps.
  4. scripts/record_demo.sh ties it together: fresh start, spawn camera, record, drive the three waypoints, stop.

15What was smart about this

The talking points, in the order an interviewer might care.

  1. Checked what the data really was. "Face 0" meant a raw scan, not a mesh, which turned "import a file" into "reconstruct a surface".
  2. Used Poisson's own density output to delete the surfaces Poisson invented across holes.
  3. Measured obstacles above the local floor, not a global plane, because SLAM floors drift.
  4. Defined a wall as vertical structure (points across many height bands), which made the map immune to ghost haze.
  5. Kept the robot inside the building: the map marks only scanned floor as free, and the planner gets a second, bounded map where everything outside the building outline is a wall.
  6. Split visual and collision geometry: pretty scan to look at, flat ground to drive on.
  7. Challenged an earlier conclusion ("origin is inside a wall") with independent evidence, and restored the spawn the assignment actually specified.
  8. Published localized position, not raw odometry, on a wall-clock timer so it's truly 10 Hz.
  9. Verified Nav2's success against the robot's measured position, which caught a real framework bug.
  10. Kept the software stack dependency-free: numpy/scipy map builder, stdlib web dashboard, OpenCV demo recorder.
  11. Made everything reproducible: scripts regenerate the map and collision mesh byte-for-byte, and one command starts the system.

16Questions you might get, with answers

Why pre-generate the map instead of projecting the 3D world live in the costmap?

The world is static, so a one-time projection gives the same information with less machinery (no simulated depth camera, no voxel layer tuning), and you can open the map and check it. AMCL only needs a correct 2D map; it doesn't care how it was made.

How does AMCL know where the robot starts?

We seed it with set_initial_pose: true at (0, 0, 0), the same place Gazebo spawns the robot. The particles start clustered there. The dashboard's "set pose" mode can re-seed it by publishing /initialpose.

What's the difference between map, odom and base_link?

map is fixed to the building. odom is where wheel odometry thinks the start was; it drifts. base_link is the robot. AMCL publishes map→odom (the drift correction), the wheels give odom→base, and robot_state_publisher gives the fixed offsets to the sensors.

Why is /robot/current_xyz published on the wall clock?

Gazebo runs below real time here (~0.8×). A sim-time timer gave 7.6 real Hz. The requirement is 10 Hz for consumers, so the timer uses the steady clock; measured 10.0–10.1 Hz.

Why PoseStamped for the waypoint?

It's exactly the goal type NavigateToPose takes (frame, position, orientation). Accepting it directly means no lossy conversion. Callers who only care about x/y leave the orientation as identity.

You removed the laser obstacle layer. Isn't that dangerous?

For this static scanned world, every obstacle is in the map, so nothing is lost. The obstacle layer froze the costmap in this WSL setup, which broke navigation entirely. On a real robot, or on a faster machine, you'd bring it back (or use a voxel layer) and investigate the deadlock. It's documented as a trade-off.

How accurate is localization?

5–16 cm versus Gazebo's ground truth at goal arrivals. The main risk is the long, featureless corridor: if AMCL were started at the wrong place it could lock on a few metres off, which is why the initial pose matters.

How would this change on a real robot?

Same software, different sources: real lidar and wheel odometry instead of Gazebo's, the obstacle layer back on for people and moving objects, the map rebuilt from the robot's own SLAM, and the velocity thresholds tied to real safety limits.

17Limits & what you'd do next

  • Unmapped obstacles are handled only in obstacle mode (section 18), through our own node. Next: fix the ObstacleLayer freeze upstream and compare the two approaches.
  • Simulation below real time (~0.8×) with the 127k-triangle collision mesh. Next: simplify walls into boxes for collision.
  • Grey mesh: colours weren't carried over. Next: bake vertex colours into the mesh for a more realistic view.
  • Corridor aliasing: the long corridor looks the same along its length to a 2D laser. Next: more particles, or add distinctive features.
  • lastmile_bot: the custom robot model is included but wasn't part of the verified runs.

18Obstacle avoidance mode

Three boxes the robot has never been told about, found with the lidar while driving.

Turn it on with OBSTACLES=1 bash scripts/run_demo.sh. Three 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.6 m boxes are spawned in a slalom in the middle of the east–west corridor, at (4.5, +0.45), (6.5, −0.45) and (10.5, +0.45). The corridor there is about 1.65 m wide, so each box leaves a gap of about 1.1 m for the 0.44 m robot.

How the lidar evidence reaches Nav2

Why evidence and not "any hit = obstacle"One stray reading shouldn't block a corridor. A cell needs two consistent scans (+3 each, threshold 6) to become an obstacle. Every beam that passes through a cell lowers its evidence, so a box that is moved away is cleared automatically. It's the same idea as the occupancy map builder: require consistency, not a single point.
Why ignore hits near mapped wallsAMCL's position is good to about 15 cm. Without a margin, the lidar would see every wall as slightly shifted and mark a thin "obstacle" beside it, narrowing the corridor. Hits within 30 cm of a known wall are treated as the wall.
Carrying the walls in /obstacle_mapThe global costmap isn't size-locked, so every new /obstacle_map message makes it re-size all its layers, and that wipes the other StaticLayer's copy of its map. So /obstacle_map carries the bounded walls from /nav_map plus the detections. That way the costmap is right no matter how the layers merge. (This bit us once: the robot planned straight out of the building until the walls were carried here too; see section 19.)

19Delivery mode

A second web page for a person at the building: pick up at A, drop off at B, then the robot goes home.

What the user does

  1. Step 1: "Where should the robot pick up?" Tap one of the 10 numbered tiles under the map, or tap the floor plan. The tile turns green with a Pickup tag, an A pin drops on the map, and step 1 gets a check.
  2. Step 2: "Where should it be delivered?" The pickup tile is locked out. The chosen drop-off turns orange (B), the page shows A → B → Dock, and Start Delivery turns blue. Each stop has a Change link; "Surprise me" picks two random places.

Keeping the robot inside the building

The 2D map marks never-scanned space as unknown, and map_server loads unknown as free (free_thresh 0.25). That's needed because some real floor was only half scanned. The catch: the scanned corridor wall has gaps, and the Gazebo world really is open past them, so the planner could route through a gap and drive around outside the building.

Two maps, two jobsscripts/make_nav_map.py takes the same cleaned building outline the delivery page draws, grows it by 35 cm (the tidied outline trims some half-scanned real floor), and marks everything outside it as a wall. That's lastmile_nav_map.pgm, served by a second map_server on /nav_map. Both costmaps plan on /nav_map; AMCL keeps localising on the honest /map, so the extra walls never distort the position estimate.
The first try didn't workWith obstacle mode on, the robot still left the building on the Library → Server Room leg. A dump of /global_costmap/costmap showed why: the /nav_map walls weren't in it. Every /obstacle_map update re-sizes the (not size-locked) global costmap, which wipes the other static layer. The fix was to have obstacle_mapper publish the /nav_map walls underneath its detections. After that, 169k lethal cells covered everything outside the building, and every logged plan stayed inside.
Then the robot got stuck turning out of the north armWith the outside walled off, the robot now has to take the real corner from the north arm into the corridor, and it hadn't done that before. The DWB controller cuts tight 90° turns. At that corner it pushed the robot's centre into the wall's inscribed zone (closer than one robot radius). NavFn can't start a path from there, and the recovery backup reported "Collision Ahead". The fix was two small round keep-outs (0.25 m) on the two wall corners at the arm's mouth, added to the planning map only. The path, and DWB with it, now take a wider turn.
Why not just switch controllers?I tried Regulated Pure Pursuit with a rotation shim, which tracks paths much more tightly. It got round the corner, but in this below-real-time simulation its turn-in-place kept swinging back and forth until the progress checker gave up. DWB was already proven on every earlier run, so it stayed, with a map-level fix instead of a controller swap. One more small change: the obstacle detector now grows boxes by 10 cm instead of 5 cm, because DWB also shaves the inside of a turn right after passing a box.

20Glossary

point cloud
A set of 3D points sampled from surfaces, with no connectivity.
mesh
Triangles joined into a surface; what simulators draw and collide with.
voxel
A small cube of space; voxel downsampling keeps one point per cube.
RANSAC
Fit a model to random minimal samples and keep the one most points agree with.
Poisson reconstruction
Builds a smooth closed surface from oriented points by solving for an inside/outside function.
SDF
Gazebo's XML format for worlds and models.
URDF
ROS's XML description of a robot's links and joints.
occupancy grid
A 2D image where each cell is free, occupied or unknown.
TF
ROS's system of coordinate frames and the transforms between them.
odometry
Position estimated by counting wheel rotation; smooth but drifts.
AMCL
Particle-filter localization against a known map.
costmap
A grid of navigation cost; walls are lethal, and cost fades out around them.
Nav2
The ROS 2 navigation stack: planner, controller, recoveries, behaviour tree.
NavigateToPose
The Nav2 action: "go to this pose", with feedback and a result.
action
A ROS request that runs over time, can report progress and be cancelled.
lifecycle node
A node with explicit states (inactive, active…) that a manager brings up in order.
cmd_vel
The velocity command topic: forward speed and turn rate.
DDS
The middleware ROS 2 uses to move messages between processes.
SSE
Server-Sent Events: a one-way live data stream from a web server to a browser.
